2026 Midterm Forecasts

Final pre-election forecasts of the U.S. Senate and House, as of 9:00 AM ET on November 3, 2026

Two chambers, one family of models, and 100,000 simulated election nights apiece. These are the final forecasts from a polling-plus-fundamentals model of the 2026 midterms: the Senate model was built by Team 5 in UMass Amherst’s DACSS program, and the House model is my own extension of it to all 435 districts. Both ran every three hours in the final days of the campaign and made their last call at 9:00 AM on Election Day.

These are frozen pre-election snapshots. For how they held up, see Forecast vs. results at the bottom of this page and the full forecast scorecard.

Model. Most_Magnificent_Model is Team 5’s final model, chosen from seven competing versions the team built and tested against one another. Each state’s forecast blends three sources by their precision:

  • Polls from VoteHub, weighted by recency (7-day half-life), sample size, voter screen (likely 1.0, registered 0.85, adults 0.7), pollster grade, and an in-cycle house-effect correction, with a penalty for pollsters that flood a single race.
  • Fundamentals, built from each state’s partisan lean and a national environment (75% generic ballot, 25% presidential approval), plus incumbency and candidate effects.
  • Kalshi race prices, corrected for longshot bias and used as one more input, not the answer.

The 100,000 simulations draw correlated national, regional, and state-level polling errors. Their sizes were calibrated against every Senate, governor, and presidential polling miss in FiveThirtyEight’s archive since 1998.

Model. The House forecast is my solo extension of the Senate model: the same polls, national environment, weighting, and Kalshi treatment, stretched across all 435 districts. The settings that don’t carry over from the Senate were re-measured on the 2018 and 2022 House midterms:

  • Fundamentals are 1.78 times each district’s 2024 presidential lean, measured on the redrawn 2026 lines (the Senate model uses 2.0). House candidates run a little closer to the middle than the top of the ticket.
  • Incumbency is worth 4.5 points, applied only where an incumbent is running in that district.
  • A state-level error (2.4 points) is added on top of the national and regional errors, because districts in the same state share a top of the ticket, turnout, and redistricting quirks.
  • Edge cases are handled one by one: uncontested and same-party top-two seats are counted as certain, independents in AK-AL and CA-06 are treated individually, and Louisiana’s all-party primary is forecast by party and lean.
